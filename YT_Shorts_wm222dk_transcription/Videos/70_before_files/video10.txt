Claims identified & fact-check:

Claim 1: People with ADHD have minds that are "moving too fast," with racing thoughts, distraction, and hyperactivity/fidgeting
→ Supported — consistent with ICD-11 inattention and hyperactivity-impulsivity symptom clusters (distractibility, excessive motor activity, restlessness)
Claim 2: Stimulant medications are the standard treatment given to people with ADHD
→ Supported — NICE guideline recommends methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine (both stimulants) as first-line pharmacological treatments for both children/young people and adults with ADHD
Claim 3: Stimulant medication works by "hyperactivating the brakes" — i.e., activating the frontal lobes, which then control/suppress other brain regions (including "emotional circuitry")
→ Partially supported — this is a reasonable lay simplification of an actual neuroscientific model: stimulants are understood to increase dopamine/norepinephrine activity in prefrontal-striatal circuits, enhancing top-down executive control (including inhibitory control) over other brain regions. This general "strengthening top-down regulation" concept is broadly consistent with mainstream neuroscience explanations of stimulant action in ADHD, though it is a simplified, non-technical framing rather than a precise clinical description, and isn't something spelled out explicitly in DSM-5-TR, ICD-11, or the NICE guideline (which describe treatment efficacy but not this specific mechanistic explanation)
Claim 4: Caffeine (tea/coffee) helps calm people with ADHD via the same "hyperactivating the frontal lobe" mechanism as prescription stimulants
→ Unsupported / overstated — caffeine is a much weaker and pharmacologically different stimulant (primarily an adenosine receptor antagonist) than methylphenidate or amphetamine-based medications (which primarily act on dopamine/norepinephrine reuptake and release). Equating the two mechanisms, and presenting caffeine as effectively delivering the same clinical "braking" effect as prescribed ADHD medication, is not supported by the reference materials and could mislead viewers into substituting caffeine for actual evidence-based treatment
