Claim 1 (title): ADHD is NOT a deficit of attention
→ Partially supported / mischaracterized — this is an oversimplification. While ICD-11 does support the idea that attention regulation (not just quantity) is central — noting people can sustain attention fully during highly stimulating activities — ADHD's official name and diagnostic criteria are explicitly built around genuine deficits in sustained attention on tasks lacking stimulation ("having difficulty sustaining attention on tasks that do not provide a high level of stimulation or reward or require sustained mental effort" is itself listed as a core essential feature). So framing it as "not a deficit of attention at all" overstates the reframe — it is a deficit in one context (low-stimulation tasks) and not another, not an absence of attentional deficit altogether

Claim 2: The person can pay attention to something they find riveting for a very long time, losing track of body, time, or surroundings (describing hyperfocus)
→ Supported — matches ICD-11's explicit note: "inattention may not be evident when the individual is engaged in activities that provide intense stimulation and frequent rewards"

Claim 3: The core issue is that attention is dysregulated — the person can't always control what their brain chooses to focus on
→ Supported — this framing aligns with ICD-11's overall conceptualization of inattention as involving difficulty sustaining and directing attention appropriately (e.g., "being easily distracted by extraneous stimuli or thoughts not related to the task at hand"), which is fundamentally a regulation/control problem rather than a simple lack of attentional capacity

Claim 4: When there's a required task (e.g., a class, doing taxes) competing with something more stimulating, it can be "almost impossible" to redirect attention to the required task
→ Partially supported — matches the substance of ICD-11's inattention criteria (difficulty sustaining attention on non-stimulating but required tasks, being distracted by other stimuli), though "almost impossible" somewhat overstates severity compared to ICD-11's language of "difficulty," which describes impairment rather than total inability
