Claims identified & fact-check:

Claim 1: Dr. Russell Barkley conducted a "light bulb experiment" studying time perception in people with and without ADHD, turning a light on/off and asking participants to estimate how long it had been on
→ Supported (externally verified) — confirmed by multiple independent sources describing this exact methodology, including a clinical trial protocol referencing the same light-bulb-based time reproduction paradigm (Hurks et al., 2011) and multiple secondary sources describing Barkley's original light bulb study design

Claim 2: Both ADHD and non-ADHD participants were able to estimate the amount of time the light was on with roughly similar accuracy (i.e., no significant difference in time estimation)
→ Supported (externally verified) — directly confirmed by a peer-reviewed Springer study co-authored by Barkley: "manifested more impaired time reproduction, but not time estimation, than did the CC [control] group," and further corroborated by a 2001 literature summary noting "most have found no deficits in time estimation"

Claim 3: When given the light switch and asked to reproduce the same time interval themselves, ADHD participants could not reliably reproduce it as accurately as non-ADHD participants (i.e., a deficit in time reproduction, not perception)
→ Supported (externally verified) — directly confirmed by the same peer-reviewed study: ADHD participants showed "more impaired time reproduction... than did the CC group," and this finding is described as having been replicated (with some failures to replicate noted) across multiple studies per the 2001 Barkley summary

Claim 4: This shows that people with ADHD are less capable of using their minds to govern their actions around time-sensitive tasks — they can perceive time to a degree, but struggle to judge/estimate how much time a task will take them to complete
→ Supported (externally verified, with a caveat) — this is Barkley's own theoretical framing (his "self-regulation" model of ADHD and time), consistent with the research findings on impaired reproduction vs. intact estimation; however, it's worth noting this is presented as Barkley's interpretive theory rather than an uncontested consensus — one source noted conclusions in this area "weren't nearly as definitive as he likes to present them" and that some studies failed to replicate the reproduction deficit, so a small degree of overstatement of certainty exists, though the core claim is fundamentally accurate
