### Claims identified & fact-check

**Claim 1:** People with ADHD often have impaired awareness of hunger and fullness signals  
→ **Supported with qualification** — altered interoceptive processing has been reported in ADHD, but the evidence is not strong enough to say that people with ADHD generally have “really poor” body awareness. Hunger and satiety awareness varies substantially between individuals.

**Claim 2:** Common stimulant medications such as Adderall and Vyvanse can suppress appetite, and relying only on hunger cues may therefore contribute to undereating  
→ **Supported** — decreased appetite is a well-established adverse effect of amphetamine-based ADHD medication. A person whose medication suppresses appetite may not receive reliable hunger cues during the medication’s active period.

**Claim 3:** Time-management difficulties and prolonged absorption in an activity can make it difficult for a person with ADHD to notice or act on hunger cues  
→ **Supported with qualification** — ADHD-related inattention, poor time awareness, and difficulty shifting between activities can interfere with regular eating. “Time blindness” and “hyperfocus” are informal descriptions rather than diagnostic criteria.

**Claim 4:** ADHD-related executive-function and prioritization difficulties can cause a person to postpone eating until hunger becomes intense  
→ **Supported with qualification** — difficulties organizing, sequencing, initiating, and switching tasks can interfere with meal timing, although this is not experienced by every person with ADHD.

**Claim 5:** A combination of reliance on routine and desire for novelty can make meal planning and preparation difficult for people with ADHD  
→ **Supported with qualification** — routines can compensate for executive difficulties, while reward- and novelty-related tendencies may affect adherence to repetitive plans. The described internal conflict is a possible experience, not a universal ADHD mechanism.

**Claim 6:** People with ADHD characteristically eat one “hyperfixation food” repeatedly and then develop a sudden aversion to it  
→ **Unsupported** — this experience is widely described online, but it is not an established ADHD characteristic and is not supported well enough to generalize to people with ADHD.

**Claim 7:** Undereating can impair attention and executive performance and make later food decisions more difficult  
→ **Supported with qualification** — inadequate energy intake and hunger can impair cognitive performance. The precise effect and severity depend on the individual and do not uniquely arise from ADHD.

**Claim 8:** The multiple executive steps involved in obtaining and preparing food can overwhelm some people with ADHD and increase reliance on convenience food or takeout  
→ **Supported with qualification** — planning, sequencing, working-memory, and task-initiation difficulties can make multistep meal preparation harder. The claimed behavioural consequence is plausible and reported, but is not inevitable.

**Claim 9:** Sounds, smells, and visual clutter during cooking can be overstimulating for people with ADHD  
→ **Supported with qualification** — sensory-processing differences occur more often in people with ADHD than in comparison groups, so cooking may be overstimulating for some. Sensory over-responsivity is not a core ADHD diagnostic criterion.

**Claim 10:** Food reliably boosts dopamine in a way that explains ADHD-related snacking and shows that the behaviour is meeting core neurological needs  
→ **Unsupported** — food engages reward systems, but the video turns a complex interaction among appetite, reward, habits, emotions, environment, and medication into a simple dopamine-deficiency explanation. A person’s snacking cannot be assigned this cause without assessment.

**Claim 11:** People with ADHD use food to initiate and complete tasks specifically because food boosts dopamine  
→ **Unsupported** — eating may be used as a reward or source of stimulation, but the proposed dopamine mechanism and its general role in ADHD task initiation have not been established.

**Claim 12:** Eating is an ADHD “stimming” behaviour that meets stimulation needs  
→ **Unsupported** — repetitive eating can serve sensory or self-regulatory functions for some individuals, but it is not established as a characteristic ADHD behaviour. The statement lacks sufficient evidence as a general ADHD claim.

**Claim 13:** Binge eating is more common among people with ADHD  
→ **Supported** — systematic reviews and meta-analyses find a significant association between ADHD and binge-eating or other disordered-eating outcomes. This does not mean that most people with ADHD binge eat or that ADHD alone causes binge eating.

Melatonin does not rise when it should, so even when the body is exhausted, the brain might not feel sleepy until very late. ### Claims identified & fact-check

**Claim 3:** A nighttime “second wind” in ADHD occurs because the brain is finally decompressing after a day of masking or imposed structure  
→ **Unsupported** — this may describe some people’s subjective experience, but it is not an established causal explanation for delayed sleep in ADHD.

→ **Supported with qualification** — morning bright-light interventions can advance circadian timing, including in adults with ADHD and delayed sleep phase. The exact natural-light dose and the stated 30–60-minute window are practical guidance rather than a universally validated prescription.

**Claim 6:** A reverse alarm exactly 60–90 minutes before bed helps the ADHD brain shift gradually out of hyperfocus  
→ **Unsupported** — an advance reminder is a reasonable behavioural prompt, but the claimed interval and ADHD-specific mechanism have not been adequately established.

**Claim 7:** A consistent, predetermined wind-down routine can reduce bedtime friction and support sleep  
→ **Supported with qualification** — consistent sleep routines and reduced evening stimulation are standard sleep-hygiene strategies. Evidence does not establish the video’s “fewer choices equals smoother transitions” formula as a distinct neurological mechanism.

**Claim 8:** Scheduling unstructured time earlier in the day reduces revenge bedtime procrastination in ADHD  
→ **Unsupported** — the strategy may help an individual, but direct evidence for this ADHD-specific causal effect is insufficient.

**Claim 9:** ADHD sleep problems often require more individualized management than simply instructing the person to go to bed earlier  
→ **Supported** — assessment may need to consider circadian delay, insomnia, sleep disorders, medication timing, comorbidities, routines, and environmental factors.
